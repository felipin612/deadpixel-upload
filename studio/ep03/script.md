## e01
The fish I pulled out of the ice was wearing my father's watch.

Not a watch like his. His. The strap was cracked where he used to fold it, and the back still had the scratch from the day he dropped it on the boat ramp. In the game, the fish just lay there on the ice, flapping in three frames of animation, and a text box came up. Catch logged. Returned to owner.

My father lost that watch in 1998. He drowned in 1999. I have never told anyone about the watch.

Let me go back to the beginning.

## e02
The disc came inside a plastic tackle box, the kind with fold-out trays. Someone left it on the hood of my car, in the parking lot at work, in the middle of a Tuesday. No note this time. Just the box.

In the bottom tray, under a bag of rubber worms that had melted together, there was a black PlayStation 2 case with a small square drawn on the front. If you have watched this channel before, you know that square. Dead Pixel Studios. The studio that worked from 2001 to 2004 and never released a single game.

On the spine, in white marker, someone had written STILLWATER. And under it, smaller: keep the hole open.

## e03
The title screen is a frozen lake at dusk. One wooden shack, one hole in the ice, and a radio playing so quietly you can't make out the song. There is no menu music. There is no new game option, either. You press start and you are already sitting on an upturned bucket, rod in hand, looking down into black water.

A single line of text sits at the bottom of the screen.

Everything in the lake was lost by someone. Fish until you find what's yours.

## e04
So I fished. And it's a good fishing game. Better than it has any right to be. The rod bends when something nibbles. The ice creaks if you shift your weight. You can hear the wind pushing against the walls of the shack behind you.

The first night I caught two perch, a pike, a boot and a bicycle bell. Every catch went into a log, with a little pencil drawing and a date. The dates were wrong. The bicycle bell said 1987. The boot said 1961.

I saved to the memory card around midnight and went to bed thinking it was a clever touch. Somebody on a tiny team had cared about the details.

There is no score in STILLWATER. No timer, no tournament, no shop. Just the lake, the shack, and the log. When you quit, the game doesn't ask if you want to save. It saves anyway, and the memory card screen shows one file with a snowflake icon and a play time that is always a little longer than you actually played.

## e05
The second night, the catch log had names.

Not mine. Bicycle bell, lost by D. Marsh, 1987. Boot, lost by R. Kell, 1961.

I looked them up. I don't know why. A D. Marsh reported a stolen bike in my county in 1987, in a newspaper archive I had to pay four dollars to search. And R. Kell went through the ice on Merrow Lake in the winter of 1961. They found him in the spring. One boot missing.

I told myself it was a coincidence. A small studio, an old local story, somebody on the team reading the same archives I was reading. That would explain a boot. It would not explain my father's watch.

## e06
There is a pause menu with one rule printed on it, the way NIGHTWALK had its rule. In STILLWATER it says: if the line goes still, cut it.

I didn't understand that at first. Lines go still all the time in fishing games. That's what waiting is.

But on the third night my line went still in a way I had never seen before. Not slack. Not tight. The float stopped bobbing in the middle of a ripple, like the water around it had frozen, and only the float knew.

## e07
I didn't cut it. Of course I didn't. I reeled.

It was heavy. Too heavy for the rod model, which bent into a shape that clipped straight through my character's arm. The text box said: something you lost.

And when it came up through the hole, it was a dog collar. Blue nylon, with a little tin bone that said Biscuit.

Biscuit was my dog when I was seven. He ran off the summer we moved house. I was the one who left the gate open, and I told my parents it was the wind.

## e08
After the collar, the hole was wider. Only a little. You could see it if you compared screenshots, and I did, because by then I was taking screenshots of everything.

Every time I pulled out something I had lost, the hole grew by a few pixels. My first Game Boy. A letter I never sent. My grandmother's ring, the one I told everyone I had never touched.

The log called every one of them returned to owner. They did not feel returned. They felt confessed.

## e09
I found STILLWATER in exactly one magazine. A winter 2003 preview, half a page, squeezed next to an advert for a snowboarding game.

Relaxing ice fishing from the small team at Dead Pixel, it said. Every lake is built from real local records.

And then the line that made me close the scan and open it again. The studio works from an old boathouse on the shore of the lake it recreates. Players near Merrow may recognise the view.

Merrow Lake is forty minutes from where I live. I have driven past it a hundred times.

## e10
There is an old retro gaming forum with a single reply about STILLWATER. It's from a user called 0303200400. The same user who warned people about NIGHTWALK.

It says: don't catch the last thing in the log. The log knows what everyone lost. Including the people who made it.

Then a second post, one minute later.

The debug map shows where they are.

## e11
The debug menu opens the same way as the others, a button code on the memory card screen. STILLWATER, build 0.8. Toggle ice. Show fish paths. Free camera. Lake map.

The lake map is a top-down grid of Merrow, with a red square for every catch. Hundreds of them. Decades of them.

And on the north shore there is one white building, with a label typed in the same plain font as the credits.

Dead Pixel Studios. 14 Merrow Road.

That was the first time I had ever seen where the studio actually was.

## e12
I went back to the catch log. At the bottom of the list, where the next catch would go, there was already an entry. Grey, not caught yet.

A small pencil drawing of a PS2 memory card. Lost by T. Rowe. 03/03/2004.

The tester. The name from the NIGHTWALK credits. The man who never came home from a night shift.

Under it, in the description field: he was walking back to the boathouse. The ice was thinner than the build said.

The hole on the screen was big enough now to swallow the bucket. When I leaned the camera forward, I could see something pale down in the water, far below the float, very still. Waiting the way you wait for a bus you know is coming.

I didn't fish that night. I sat with the controller in my hands and watched the float not move.

## e13
On the last night, the float went still the moment the line touched the water. Nothing else in the lake moved. The wind sound stopped. The radio stopped.

And the text box came up on its own, without me pressing anything.

You know what is on the line.

I knew. I pressed square to cut it. Nothing happened. I pressed it again and again. The rod started to bend by itself, slowly, the way something heavy comes up from very deep water.

I pulled the power cable out of the wall.

The screen went black. But for a second, before the picture collapsed, I saw the memory card screen. One file. A snowflake icon. Play time: one minute longer than I had played.

## e14
The next morning I drove to Merrow Lake.

There is a boathouse on the north shore. Boarded windows, a sagging dock, a mailbox with no name. Number fourteen.

The ice was thick enough to walk on, and someone had already cut a hole about forty feet out. Perfectly round. Still open. Next to it, on an upturned bucket, someone had left a memory card.

I haven't picked it up.

If you know anything about 14 Merrow Road, tell me in the comments. And subscribe, because I think the next disc is already on its way. I think it's waiting for whoever lost it.
