## e01
The train that wasn't on the timetable stopped outside my house.

Not a house like mine. Mine. The blue door with the paint coming off at the bottom. The porch light I keep meaning to fix, the one that flickers twice before it stays on. In the game, the train just sat there with its engine running, six dark carriages and one lit window, and a text box came up at the bottom of the screen. Platform clear. Waiting for you.

There are no train tracks within forty miles of my street. There never have been.

Let me go back to the beginning.

## e02
The disc came in my mailbox, folded inside an old paper railway timetable. No envelope, no stamp, no note. Just the timetable, soft from being opened and closed too many times, with one departure circled in pencil.

Inside the fold there was a black PlayStation 2 case with a small square drawn on the front. If you have watched this channel before, you know that square. Dead Pixel Studios. The studio that worked from 2001 to 2004 and never released a single game.

On the spine, in white marker, someone had written SIGNALMAN. And under it, smaller: keep the line clear.

## e03
The title screen is a signal box at night. A small wooden hut on stilts beside a single railway line, one lamp in the window, rain coming down in thin grey streaks. No music. Just rain on the roof and, somewhere far off, a bell.

You press start and you are already inside, standing in front of a row of iron levers, with a big painted diagram of the line on the wall above them.

A single line of text sits at the bottom of the screen.

Clear only the trains on the timetable. Anything else, hold at red.

## e04
So I worked the box. And it's a good game. Calm, slow, strangely satisfying. A bell rings when a train is offered to you from the next box down the line. You ring back to accept it. You pull the levers in the right order, the points click across, the signal arm drops to green, and a little low-poly freight train rolls past your window with its lamps swinging.

The first night I cleared the ten past ten freight, the twenty to midnight mail, and two empty coal trains. Every one of them was printed on the timetable pinned beside the levers. Every one of them came exactly on time.

When you quit, the game doesn't ask if you want to save. It saves anyway. The memory card screen shows one file with a little lamp icon, and a play time that is always a few minutes longer than I actually played.

## e05
The second night, at three minutes past three in the morning, a light came on in the diagram.

Not on any track I had worked. On a short grey line at the very edge of the board, a branch that went off the painted map and stopped at the frame. I hadn't noticed it before. The light was red, which meant something was standing on that section, waiting.

Then the bell rang. Three beats, a pause, three beats. I checked the bell codes taped to the wall. Three, pause, three isn't on the list.

I checked the timetable. There is no train at three minutes past three.

So I did what the game told me. I left the signal at red.

## e06
It waited. All night. The light just sat there on the edge of the board, and every few minutes the bell rang again. Three, pause, three. I let the game run while I made coffee and it was still ringing when I came back.

When the sun came up in the game, the light went out by itself, and the train register printed one new line. Held at signal: oh three oh three. Driver: J. Kell.

I didn't know that name. But I have read enough about this studio now to know that nothing in their games is random. Every name is somebody. So I started looking.

## e07
The third night, the train came closer.

It was out of the box now, the first section of the real line, and from the window I could see it for the first time. One engine. Six carriages. All of them dark except the last one, where a single window was lit yellow.

There was someone sitting in that window. Low-poly, just a few grey shapes, but sitting very straight, the way you sit when you are waiting for someone to notice you. They had something in their lap. Small, square, grey.

I zoomed the camera in as far as the game would let me. It was a memory card.

## e08
That was the night I found the magazine.

A preview in a gaming magazine from the summer of 2002, half a page near the back. Signalman. A relaxing railway simulation from a small new studio. There's one screenshot of the lever frame and a short quote from the founder about growing up next to a branch line that closed when they were a child.

The founder's name was printed under the quote. J. Kell.

The preview ends with a sentence I have read maybe fifty times now. Every train in Signalman is real. Every one of them ran, once.

## e09
There is an old forum thread about Dead Pixel, from 2007. I have quoted it before. It's mostly people guessing. But near the bottom of the second page there's a reply I had always skipped, because it was just one line.

Kell's dad drowned in Merrow Lake in sixty-one. She started the studio in his boathouse. Never got over it.

If you saw the last episode, you know that name. R. Kell went through the ice on Merrow Lake in the winter of 1961. One boot missing. And now I know who started Dead Pixel Studios. His daughter. J. Kell.

## e10
I went into the debug menu. Same button combination as the other Dead Pixel games. Most of the options are broken. One of them still works. It's called LINE VIEW.

It shows the whole railway from above, every box, every station, every siding. And it shows the branch at the edge of the map, the one that wasn't painted on the diagram.

It doesn't end at the frame. It keeps going. Off the old branch line, across a frozen lake, past a white boathouse on the north shore, and on through a town I know very well. It ends at a station with a name I had to read three times.

It's the name of my street.

## e11
The fourth night, I didn't even play. I just left the game on, the signal at red, and watched.

At three minutes past three the bell rang. Three, pause, three. And the train moved. Not because I cleared it. The signal never changed. It just crept forward, a few metres at a time, until its lamps filled the window of the signal box.

A text box came up, on its own, with nobody pressing anything.

Holding a train is not the same as stopping it.

The person in the last window had turned their head. They were looking straight up at the box. At me.

## e12
I didn't sleep after that. I sat with the timetable that came with the disc, the paper one, and I looked again at the one departure that someone had circled in pencil.

Three minutes past three. Merrow Road. Platform one.

On the back, in the same white marker as the spine, someone had written a single line. If you hold it, it waits for you. If you clear it, it goes home.

I don't know who wrote that. I think I know who is on that train.

## e13
The fifth night, I pulled the lever.

The signal arm dropped to green. The points clicked across. The bell rang one last time, a single beat, which in the codes on the wall means train entering section. And the three oh three rolled past my window, slowly, every carriage dark now except the last.

The window was empty. The memory card was on the seat.

Then the screen went black. When it came back, it was the memory card browser. One file. SIGNALMAN. Created 03/03/2004.

## e14
I'm recording this at my kitchen table. It's a quarter past three in the morning.

About twelve minutes ago I heard a train. A real one. A horn, far off, and then the long sound of wheels on rail, very close, right outside, where there is nothing but my lawn and a street with no tracks for forty miles.

I haven't opened the front door yet. But I can see through the glass that the porch light is on. It flickered twice, and then it stayed on.

There's something on the doormat. Small, square, grey.

If you know anything about J. Kell, tell me in the comments. And if you have a Dead Pixel disc of your own, keep the line clear. Subscribe, because I'm going to open the door.
